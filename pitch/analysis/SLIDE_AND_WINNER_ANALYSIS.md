## What the rules actually reward

Official 2026 rules: https://www.congressionalappchallenge.us/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/2026-CAC-Rules.pdf

Required demonstration contents: participant names, app name, clear purpose sentence, intended audience, tools and coding languages, and working functionality. Public YouTube/Vimeo, 1–3 minutes. Judging includes quality/originality, implementation/user experience and programming skills. There is no required slide count or required investor-style market/traction section. AI usage must be fully disclosed and the student's significant contribution/technical understanding must be genuine.

**Editorial implication:** keep a small number of visuals that explain, then show the running app. A beautiful repeated feature list does not supply additional evidence of functionality. The video should show meaningful interaction, not just scrolling across tabs.

## Honest diagnosis of our deck

The master deck solved “include everything” but is not a good three-minute sequence. Its largest problem is duplication, not the navy/mint design.

- Slides 4, 6, 10 and 11 re-explain the feature set.
- Slides 12, 13 and 14 introduce/plan the demo three times. Only 12 should appear, briefly, before actual app footage.
- Slides 15, 16 and 20 repeat choice, trust, local data and evidence boundaries.
- There is too much abstract positioning before app use. One user-facing action has more evidentiary value than another statement about calmness.
- The games are clear on slide 7. Keep them central; show at least one complete interaction loop.
- Research is important but does not need a full slide of caveats in a short video. Keep a source note and one honest line: research-informed design, not validated therapy.
- Slide 3 is visually strong but the statistics mix age groups and do not establish a shared clinical need. Autism prevalence is not evidence that autistic people are isolated or need treatment. Simplify to one directly relevant fact, or use a truthful personal problem statement. The CDC figure concerns identified eight-year-olds, not all children generally. The WHO adherence estimate is old and should be dated if retained.
- Stack/framework lists, lines of code and release counts are not substitutes for explaining programming. State the languages clearly and give one comprehensible coding solution.

## What the 80-second demonstration should prove

Prioritize three coherent mini-workflows over eight disconnected features:

1. **Play and choose (~35s):** Pattern Recall: watch → repeat → continue/replay. Reveal two Picture Pairs cards. Start and stop rain. Explain user control, no timer/score. Put the other activity names in the game overview; it is unnecessary to play every game on camera.
2. **Manage the day (~15s):** choose a mood → complete a routine → show a medication self-check-in if readable. Distinguish self-report from verified medication administration.
3. **Connect and prepare (~30s):** show a Coffee Circle's real join option (no need to wait in a live room); open visit brief → choose fields → show exact preview. Keep notes local and sharing deliberate. If a real Jitsi room is demonstrated, use a consenting participant and do not expose private information.

This sequence explains the breadth of the app while giving games most of the opening attention. It does not pretend that a static screenshot, room link or local note proves live collaboration.
